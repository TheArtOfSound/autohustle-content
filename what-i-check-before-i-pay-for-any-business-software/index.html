What I Check Before I Pay for Any Business Software

Published: November 28, 2026 · By Bryan Leonard, Qira LLC / Ah Crap Cleanup — Chandler, AZ

I run a pet-waste cleanup business, a small agency, and a handful of software projects out of the same laptop. Between them I have tried a lot of tools, and I pay for a few. Every tool that gets my card passes the same five checks first. None of them are clever. All of them have saved me money, usually by stopping me from paying for something I did not need yet, or from getting locked into something I could not leave.

The checks below are in the order I run them. A tool that fails check one never gets to check five.

1. Use the free tier until it actually hurts

The first check is the simplest: does the free tier do the job I have today, not the job I imagine having in six months? I run my bookings, reminders, and follow-ups on free tools — Gmail with labels, Google Calendar, and a plain spreadsheet for the lead list — and they carry the whole business. The day the spreadsheet starts costing me real time is the day I pay for a CRM, not before.

The trap is paying for capacity you do not use yet. A tool that charges per seat, per contact, or per automation run will happily take your money while you are still small enough that the free tier would have done. I set a rule for myself: I only upgrade when I can point at a specific thing the free tier cannot do that is costing me a customer or an hour a week. "It would be nice" is not a reason. "I lost a lead because of it" is.

One honest exception: if the free tier is so limited it cannot even show me whether the tool works — a trial that hides the one feature I need to test — I treat that as a failed check and move on. A tool that will not let me try it properly is telling me something about how it treats customers.

2. Test the checkout before you need it

If a tool takes payments from my customers, or takes my payment, I want to see the checkout work before anything depends on it. I learned this wiring up checkouts for my own products: a payment page that looks fine in the dashboard can still fail at the last step, and the only way to know is to run a real test through it.

For a tool I am buying, that means reading the refund policy before I pay, and checking that the receipt and the billing page actually exist. If I cannot find where to see my invoices or change my card, I do not sign up. For a tool that will take my customers' money, I run a test purchase myself — a one-dollar item, a test card, whatever the tool offers — and I confirm the confirmation email arrives and the money lands where it should. A checkout that works is a feature. A checkout that fails on launch day is a lost customer and a refund conversation.

3. Can I get my data out?

Before I put anything real into a tool, I look for the export button. Customer lists, job history, notes, templates — if there is no way to download my own data in a usable format, I do not build on it. I have seen too many tools hold a business hostage with the data the business itself created.

The check takes thirty seconds: open settings, look for export, download, or backup. If it is there, try it once and open the file to make sure it is real data and not a broken export. If it is not there, or it is buried behind a sales call, that is a no. The tool is not selling me software at that point. It is selling me a room with no door.

This one matters double for anything that holds customer contact details. My lead list is the business. A tool that will not give it back is a tool I do not trust with it in the first place.

4. Price the next tier, not just this one

The price on the pricing page is the price for the business I have today. The price that matters is the one I will pay when the business grows. Before I sign up I read the next tier up and do the math: what happens when I add a second person, when I pass the contact limit, when I need the feature that is only on the plan above mine?

I have passed on tools whose starter plan was cheap and whose next step tripled the bill for one feature I would need within months. The honest version of the price is the one you will pay at the size you are aiming for, not the size you are now. If the jump is steep, I either plan for it or I pick a tool whose pricing grows with me instead of against me.

Same logic applies to annual billing. A tool that offers 20 percent off for paying a year upfront is offering me a discount for a commitment I have not earned yet. I pay monthly until the tool has proven itself for at least two or three months. The annual discount is still there later. The money I would have locked in is not.

5. Find the cancel button before you pay

The last check is the one most people skip: before I enter a card, I look for how I would leave. Can I cancel from the settings page, or do I have to email support, or — worst of all — get on a call? If cancellation takes more than a couple of minutes, I do not sign up.

This is not about planning to quit. It is about knowing the exit exists. A tool that makes it easy to leave is a tool that has to earn the renewal every month, and those are the tools I keep. A tool that hides the exit is telling me it plans to keep me with friction instead of value.

I also check what happens to my data when I cancel. Does the account stay readable for a while, or does everything vanish the day the subscription ends? Combined with check three, this tells me the real cost of trying the tool: it should be the monthly price, nothing more.

If you are about to pay for a tool, run the five checks in order and be honest about the results. Most tools fail at least one, and the failure usually tells you more than the sales page does. The tools that pass all five are the ones still on my card six months later — and there are not many of them, which is exactly the point.

Frequently asked questions

Do you really run your business on free tools?
Most of it, yes. Gmail with labels, Google Calendar, and a spreadsheet carry bookings, reminders, and the lead list for Ah Crap Cleanup. I pay for tools only when I can point at a specific job the free tier cannot do that is costing me a customer or an hour a week.

What if a tool has no free tier, only a paid trial?
Then checks two through five matter more, and I read the refund and cancellation terms before the trial starts. If I cannot cancel from settings and cannot find a refund policy, I pass. A trial that is hard to leave is not a trial.

Is annual billing ever worth it?
Yes, but only after the tool has proven itself on monthly billing for two or three months. The annual discount is still there later. Locking in a year of a tool I have not lived with yet is a bet, not a saving.
