How I Run Bookings for a One-Person Pet-Waste Cleanup Business (Without Missing a Lead)

Published: November 22, 2026 · By Bryan Leonard, Ah Crap Cleanup — Chandler, AZ

The problem with a phone-based service business

I run Ah Crap Cleanup, a pet-waste cleanup service in Chandler, Arizona, serving Chandler, Gilbert, Queen Creek, and Mesa. Most customers first reach out by text, at whatever hour they happen to be standing in their yard looking at the mess. If a text sits unanswered for a day, that customer has already found someone else. So the whole booking system is built around one rule: every inquiry gets seen, priced, and answered fast — and nothing lives only in my head.

This is the exact setup I use. It costs almost nothing beyond the tools themselves, and it works for any one- or two-person field service business: lawn care, junk hauling, window cleaning, mobile detailing.

1. One business number for everything

Every customer contacts the same number: our Google Voice line. It is not my personal cell. That separation matters for two reasons. First, customers get a consistent number from the website, the Facebook page, and the yard signs. Second, Google Voice forwards every text to my email, so a message is never trapped on a phone I left in another room — it lands in the same inbox I check all day.

The practical effect: a customer can text at 9 PM, and I can read it, price it, and reply from anywhere. No missed-call voicemail black hole, no "sorry, I never saw that" — the number is the front door, and the email forward is the backup.

2. Quote from photos, not from a phone call

The first thing I ask a new customer is to text photos of the yard. That one habit replaced most of the back-and-forth. A photo tells me in ten seconds what a phone call takes five minutes to describe: yard size, how overgrown it is, how many dogs, gate access.

From the photos I give a flat rate up front. Recent jobs have ranged from about $125 for a small one-time cleanup to $350 flat for a big overgrown-yard reset. The customer knows the price before I drive out there, and I know the job is worth the trip. No hourly meter running, no surprise at the end, no haggling on the driveway.

If the photos don't show enough — a locked gate, a side yard I can't see — I say so and ask for one more angle instead of guessing. A quote I'm unsure about is worse than a slow quote.

3. Every confirmed booking goes on one shared board

The moment a customer agrees to a booking — a day, a time, a price — it goes on our shared ops board in Discord, in a channel the whole team can see. Name, service, agreed price, agreed time. Nothing else. Quotes still waiting on a reply and general questions stay out of that channel; it holds confirmed work only, so anyone opening it sees the real schedule at a glance.

That single habit is what keeps a two-person operation from double-booking. My operations manager sees the same list I see. If I'm on a roof — or asleep, or driving — the day's work is still written down where the team can find it. A booking that lives only in a text thread is a booking that can get lost.

Customer photos ride along with the booking post, so whoever shows up at the gate already knows what the yard looks like.

4. Recurring routes pay the bills; one-time jobs fill the gaps

The business has two kinds of work, and they play different roles. Weekly recurring cleanups — the same yards, the same day each week, like a Saturday route — are the base income. They are predictable, they compound, and routing them together keeps drive time down.

One-time cleanups — a yard that got away from someone, a move-out reset, a pre-party cleanup — pay more per visit and fill the gaps between route days. I take them, but I never let them push a recurring customer off the schedule. The route is the business; the one-offs are the bonus.

5. Take card payments; keep the money side boring

We take card payments through Stripe, on its own account for this business, separate from my other ventures. Customers pay the agreed flat rate; there is no invoice chase, no "I'll Venmo you later." Keeping each business on its own payment account is the kind of boring bookkeeping that saves a real headache at tax time.

6. The website form is the second front door

Not everyone wants to text. The booking form on ahcrapcleanup.com asks for the same things the text thread does — name, service needed, email, and the yard details — and every submission lands in the same inbox as the texts. One inbox, two doors. A lead that comes in through the form gets the same fast photo-quote treatment as a text.

The daily routine, in five minutes

  1. Morning triage: open the inbox, answer every overnight text and form submission first. New inquiries before anything else.
  2. Price from photos: any new yard photos get a flat-rate quote the same morning.
  3. Confirm and post: agreed bookings go on the shared board immediately — price, time, and photos included.
  4. Work the route: recurring yards in route order, one-time jobs slotted around them.
  5. Close the loop: payment taken on the day, and the next recurring visit already on the calendar.

What I would not do again

  • Don't quote by phone alone. Without photos you are guessing, and a wrong guess either loses money or loses the customer.
  • Don't keep bookings in your head or in one text thread. The day you forget one is the day a customer waits on a locked gate for a cleaner who never comes.
  • Don't mix business money with other ventures. Separate accounts per business, from the start.
  • Don't promise a time you haven't written down. "I'll be there Thursday" is not a booking until it's on the board.

FAQ

Do customers actually text photos of their yard?

Yes — it is the easiest thing in the world for them, and it makes the quote faster for both sides. Most people would rather send three photos than describe their backyard on the phone.

Why flat rates instead of hourly?

A customer can say yes to a number. They cannot say yes to a meter. Flat rates also reward working efficiently instead of punishing it.

Why Discord for the ops board?

Because the whole team is already there, it works on every phone, and a channel that holds confirmed bookings only is trivially easy to read. Any shared list the whole team actually opens would do the same job.

Does this work outside pet waste?

The shape transfers to any small field service: one number, photo-first quotes, flat rates, one shared schedule, card payment on the day. The details change; the discipline doesn't.
