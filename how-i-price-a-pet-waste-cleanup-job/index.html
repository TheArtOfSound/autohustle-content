How I Price a Pet-Waste Cleanup Job (and Why Flat Rates Beat Hourly)

Published: November 25, 2026 · By Bryan Leonard, Qira LLC / Ah Crap Cleanup — Chandler, AZ

I run Ah Crap Cleanup, a pet-waste cleanup service in the East Valley. Every job starts the same way: a customer texts me photos of their yard, and I have to give them a price before I have ever set foot on the property. Get the price wrong and I either lose the booking or lose money doing the work. Here is exactly how I price a job, with the real numbers from real jobs.

Short version: I quote a flat rate, sized to the actual work in the photos, and I never quote hourly anymore. Here is why, and how I land on the number.

1. Photos first, price second

I do not quote from a description. "The yard is pretty bad" tells me nothing — one person's "pretty bad" is another person's Saturday afternoon. Before I give any number, I ask for photos of the whole area: wide shots of each section of the yard, not close-ups of a single pile. Close-ups hide the scale; wide shots show it.

If the photos leave it genuinely unclear what work is being asked for — say, a patch of ground I cannot tell is gravel or dirt, or a side yard with no sense of size — I ask for one more photo or a little more detail before quoting. Guessing at that point is how you end up on site doing twice the work you quoted for. If the ask is clear from the photos, I quote and close the booking on the spot. Speed matters: a customer who has to wait a day for a price is a customer who has already texted the next company.

2. What I am actually pricing

A cleanup price is not a price per pile. It is a price for time on site, and time on site comes down to four things I read from the photos:

  • Area. Whole yard or one side yard? A full yard is a different job from two small side-yard strips, even if both look messy.
  • Density. How much waste is actually down there, and how long has it been building up? A yard that has not been touched in months takes longer than a weekly yard, even at the same size.
  • Ground. Grass, gravel, and dirt clean at different speeds. Gravel is the slowest — waste hides between the stones and you work section by section. Turf and grass are faster.
  • Access and obstacles. Gates, dogs on site, toys and furniture in the way, a side yard you have to carry everything through. Anything that slows the walk adds minutes.

I price the job I can see, not the job I hope it is. If the photos show a yard I can clear in about half an hour of hand-pulling, that is a small job and it gets a small price. If they show a whole yard that has been neglected for months, that is a big job and it gets a big price. The photos do the pricing; I just read them honestly.

3. Real numbers from real jobs

Two jobs from the same week show the range:

Neither price was a guess and neither was a formula. The $75 job was small, contained work I could see clearly in the photos. The $350 job was a whole yard that needed real time. The customer in both cases got the number fast, knew exactly what they were paying before I arrived, and booked.

Notice the spread: $75 to $350 for the same service, because the work is genuinely different. A price list with one number for "a cleanup" would be wrong for both of them.

4. Why flat rates beat hourly

I used to think hourly was the safe option — if the job takes longer, I get paid more. In practice it cost me bookings, for three reasons:

  • Customers hate open-ended prices. "Somewhere between $60 and $200" is not a price, it is a worry. A flat number lets someone say yes in one text.
  • Hourly punishes efficiency. The faster and better I get at the work, the less I earn per job. A flat rate rewards getting good at the job instead of punishing it.
  • Hourly creates an argument on site. Every extra ten minutes becomes a negotiation. A flat rate is agreed before I arrive, so the only conversation on site is about the work.

The risk of a flat rate is underpricing a job that turns out worse than the photos showed. I handle that by pricing what I can see and asking for more detail when the photos are unclear — not by switching to hourly. In practice, good photos plus an honest read have kept the misses rare, and the bookings I win by quoting a firm number fast are worth far more than the occasional job that runs long.

5. What I do not do

Two lines I hold even when it costs me a booking. First, I pull weeds and waste by hand — I do not spray. If a customer asks for spraying, I say so plainly and do not take the job under false pretenses. Second, I do not quote a job I cannot see. No photos, no firm price — I will give a range and a clear next step, but I will not lock in a number blind.

Both rules cost me a little work in the short term. Both rules are why the customers who do book trust the price they were given, and why they text me again the next time the yard needs it.

If you run a service business and you are still quoting hourly, try this for a week: ask for photos, price the job you can see, and give one flat number. You will close faster, argue less, and get better at reading a job from a photo — which is the skill that makes the whole system work.

Frequently asked questions

What if the job turns out bigger than the photos showed?
It happens, rarely. I finish the work I quoted and I am honest with the customer about what I found — most of the time the difference is small enough to absorb. The bookings I win by quoting fast and firm are worth far more than the occasional long job.

Do you charge extra for dogs on site or a locked gate?
Access issues go into the price I read from the photos and the first conversation. I ask about dogs, gates, and obstacles before I quote, so there are no surprises on either side.

Why not just use a standard price list?
Because a standard list is wrong for almost every job. A $75 side yard and a $350 whole yard are the same service on paper and completely different jobs in person. The photos tell me which one I am looking at.
